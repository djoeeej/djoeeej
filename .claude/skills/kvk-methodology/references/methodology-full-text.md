Preface
This is a methodological publication is intended for students and lecturers of the Faculty of
Business of Klaipėdos valstybinė kolegija | Higher Education Institution. Its purpose is to help
students prepare methodically orderly written work and to establish uniform requirements for the
preparation and assessment of written student work. These methodological recommendations and
established rules will help in proper preparation of papers, essays, reflections, reports, projects,
articles, coursework, graduation papers for pedagogical studies, and graduation papers, as well as
understand their specifics and structure. The instructions and requirements for students’ written work
specified in this methodology are mandatory. However, departments can take a creative approach in
the application of this methodology taking into account the results and specifics of the study
programmes they are implementing.
The compilers of the methodology did not seek to present an absolutely complete
methodology for writing student papers. Students and their advisers need to apply the methodological
innovations on their own, using the knowledge and skills acquired during lectures, especially in
applied research methodology, marketing research, management, language culture, document
management, information technology and other subjects.

1. GENERAL PROVISIONS FOR WRITING COURSE PAPERS, GPPS, AND
GRADUATION PAPERS AT THE FACULTY OF BUSINESS
Students writing papers must comply with the academic integrity and other ethical
requirements, specified in the documents of Klaipėdos valstybinė kolegija | Higher Education
Institution, as provided for in the Code of Academic Ethics of Klaipėdos valstybinė kolegija | Higher
Education Institution.
Throughout the course of studies, student abilities to work with scientific literature, use
academic language, and analyse and systematize information are shaped through various written
assignments. They develop student abilities to conduct an independent applied research. During their
studies, students write various works, scheduled in the study programme, i.e. papers, essays, articles,
course papers, projects/integrated projects, reports, and upon graduation, they write a professional
bachelor’s graduation paper (thesis). A student’s academic writing experience begins with the
development of independent work skills in various independent written assignments. Any of the
papers must comply with the text layout requirements set out in the methodological requirements
herein (Section 3). The structural parts of all written works must include: a title page, a summary, a
table of contents, a list of tables and figures, an introduction, a development of the topic, conclusions,
sources of literature and other information, and annexes.
All course descriptions always indicate the hours allocated for the students’ independent
work and provides more precise methodological requirements for the preparation. The scope of each
independent work depends on the learning outcomes and scope of the study subject.
1.1. Types of papers
This subsection discuses aspects of the most common types of individual papers.
Summary report A summary report is a concise form of presenting information heard, seen,
or read. A brief summary of what is most important can be done both orally and in writing (Kardelis,
2016). A summary report is an independent student work, the purpose of which is to provide a detailed
examination of a specific theoretical issue, based on a discussion of scientific literature. Reports are
among the first student assignments that contain elements of scientific work (Kardelis, 2016).
One of the keys of a summary report is to present the material in a concentrated, clear,
purposeful and informative manner. A summary report can present an overview of theoretical
material or empirical research, and analyse one or several important aspects of the issue raised. The
most important criterion for the quality of a report is that the material presented is objective,
purposeful, and informative (Kardelis, 2016).

--- PDF page 7 ---
Writing a summary report aims to develop student abilities to gather information on a chosen
topic, analyse it, systematize it, and summarize it. By writing summary report, students develop the
ability to identify scientific issues and make proposals to solve them. A summary report examines a
certain topic (issue) based on various literary sources. The topic of a summary report can be chosen
by students from a list provided by the lecturer, assigned to students by the lecturer, or proposed by
students themselves, after consulting with the lecturer. When examining the research issue, students
systematize sources of information and present their conclusions.
Summary reports are written using topical and scientific literature, legal acts, statistical and
other sources of information. This type of a paper must be based on at least 4-10 sources of literature
(recommended not only in Lithuanian, but also foreign languages).
Summary reports can be individual or delivered by a group (in the latter case, indicating the
contribution of each student). The recommended length of an individual summary report is 10–20
pages (excluding appendices). The length of group summary reports is longer, defined by the lecturer.
Essay. An essay (French: essai – an attempt) is a structured and coherent, easy-to-read,
short-form, argumentative academic paper. An essay is a piece of a student’s written work, defined
by a lecturer, the preparation of which helps develop scientific thinking and formulate statements.
Questions raised in an essay can have more than one answer, while statements and issues may tackle
completely different approaches.
Students writing an essay must provide a clear and reasoned critical view of the statements
presented and issues examined in the scientific literature, identifying their advantages and
disadvantages, proposing solutions, predicting future research directions, and inviting a discussion.
Essays help to reveal the students’ creative potential in combining scientific insights (and in some
cases, personal experience) regarding the topic in question.
Although the essay structure is quite loose, the following parts are still necessary:
introduction (raising the problem, intrigue), development of the topic (dividing the issue into parts,
argumentation, supporting it with quotes, images), conclusions (main emphasis), literature and other
sources of information, and annexes. Essays are subject to the same requirements for formatting and
work structure as reports, but the scope of a scientific essay may be smaller (the specific scope is
determined by the lecturer).
Report. A report is a written presentation of the works accomplished. Students usually write
reports about their internships. Internships can be cognitive, educational, professional, or final. They
take place at companies/institutions or practice enterprises. During internships, students perform tasks
and assignments according to the methodological and/or internship supervisor’s instructions,
applying the theoretical knowledge acquired during their studies in practice. The goal of an internship
is to develop the ability to relate theoretical knowledge to tasks performed in practical activities and

--- PDF page 8 ---
observed phenomena. An internship report is a written paper presenting a student’s internship and
describing the tasks accomplished. The purpose of an internship report is to develop skills in
describing observed organizational activities, processes, and phenomena.
Writing an internship report involves using all kinds of information, student’s individual
observations, and other experience of the internship. Individual statements are illustrated with specific
examples of the organization’s activities, documents, calculations, interviews with employees, and
process observation results.
When describing the work results, it is necessary to present them in a visual form: graphs,
tables, pictures, formulas, numbers, statistical data. It should be noted that the information presented
in tables and figures should not be repeating. Advanced students can present their calculations on
opportunities to improve the company’s performance. The scope of the internship report depends on
the credits allocated for the internship – it is specified in the internship subject description and
methodological requirements of the specific study program. The recommended length of an
individual report is 10–15 pages (excluding appendices).
Reflection. A reflection is an independent student paper, the purpose of which is to help
them rethink their activities and understand what was important and useful, what knowledge and
skills they have acquired, and what skills they still need to strengthen in order to achieve better results.
A reflection can be written after listening to a certain topic of a study subject, an entire subject, or
after completing educational, professional, etc. internship. A Reflection includes both a description
of the experience (tasks performed, what and was learned and how, how did the student feel), and
reflection on the learning experience (what went well and what didn’t, what could be done better next
time, what difficulties or problems were encountered, how did the student solve them).
The lecturer teaching the subject should provide questions for reflection that will help to
better assess the activity, experience, and usefulness, for example: What were my goals? Did I manage
to achieve them? What have I learned? What tasks have I completed? What was useful? What
problems did I encounter? How did I solve them? What knowledge and abilities do I need to improve?
What are the ways and how should I do it? Has my attitude towards this subject changed? Why?
The specific scope and questions for the independent work presented in the form of a
reflection are provided by the lecturer teaching the study subject (the suggested scope is 1–5 pages).
Project. A project/integrated project is a type of applied research work, similar to a course
paper in scope and structure, which is based not only on theoretical studies, but also on practical
experience. The starting point of any project is a specific problem in a relevant field. The exact
requirements for the structure and scope of a project are specified by the lecturer.
Article. An article is a structured, coherent, small-scale academic paper of a theoretical
and/or practical nature introducing research results or a valuable analysis of research results

--- PDF page 9 ---
conducted by scientists. An article can be written in Lithuanian or English language. Precise
requirements for articles are usually provided in conference or article publication requirements. The
length of an article is usually 5-7 pages.
A course paper is an independent research paper that presents and solves theoretical,
applied or practical problems, but its scope is relatively smaller than that of a graduation paper/final
thesis. Coursework can be integrated into a study subject or treated as a separate subject. If the course
paper is integrated into a study subject, its writing and assessment are organized by the subject
lecturer, following the subject description. If the course paper is treated as a separate study subject,
the writing and assessment process is organized by the head of the department or an assigned lecturer.
Purpose of a course paper – to develop the student ability to use scientific literature,
systematise, analyse and examine scientific and practical issues, applying proper research methods in
order to obtain applicable results.
The graduation paper of pedagogical studies is a part of pedagogical, psychological and
subject didactics studies, integrating theory and practice; it is an original, independently prepared,
non-degree qualification paper, encompassing the theoretical and practical professional training of
the future educator. It must be based on modern educational theories and a modern educational
environment. In accordance with the Order of the Minister of Education and Science of the Republic
of Lithuania On the Approval of the Provisions for of the Group of Education and Training Study
Fields (https://www.e-tar.lt), the graduation paper of pedagogical studies is written by students of all
pedagogy study programmes, offered at KVK.
The purpose of the graduation paper of pedagogical studies (hereinafter referred to as the
GPPS) is to demonstrate the achieved study results by collecting, systematizing, reflexively and
critically analysing, integrating and applying theoretical, practical, experiential, and empirical
information about the objects and subjects of pedagogical activity.
A graduation paper is an original paper, independently written by a student with the aim to
demonstrate the achieved study results by systematizing, reflectively and critically analysing,
integrating and applying theoretical, practical, experiential, and empirical information. This paper
must be publicly defended (KVK Procedure for Writing, Defence, and Storage of Graduation Papers).
The purpose of the graduation paper is to establish the level of student achievement of the
study outcome, acquired during the studies to acquire vocational qualifications and vocational
practice.
The purpose of organising the assessment of the graduation paper (final project) is to
enable the student to prove that he or she has achieved the study objectives, and determine if he or
she can be awarded with vocational qualifications.

1.2. General provisions for course papers, GPPS and graduation papers
Students of all study programs of the Faculty of Business write course papers (students of
study programs in the field of pedagogy write graduation papers of pedagogical studies). A study
programme is regarded as completed upon completion, defence, and evaluation of the graduation
paper or a graduation paper of pedagogical studies.
The writing, defence and evaluation of the graduation paper are subject to the General
Requirements for the Conduct of Studies, the descriptions of study fields approved by the Ministry
of Education, Science, and Sport, KVK study regulations, KVK Code of Academic Ethics, KVK
Procedure for the Assessment of Study Achievements, KVK Procedure for the Submission and
Examining of Appeals and Complaints, KVK Description of Plagiarism Prevention Procedure, KVK
Procedure for the Management of Intellectual Property, KVK Procedure for Writing, Defence, and
Storage of Graduation Papers, and the methodological instructions for writing papers for the studies,
indicated herein.
Student/graduate duties. Students/graduates are free to choose the object and topic of their
course paper, graduation paper or GPPS (a more detailed discussion about selecting the topic is
provided in subsection 2.1. Selecting the topic). The responsibility for the quality of the course paper,
GPPS, or graduation paper and compliance with formal requirements, correct data, calculations, and
independent research falls on the student writing the paper alone. Students writing a course paper, a
graduation paper, or a GPPS, must adhere to academic integrity and other ethical requirements
defined in the Code of Ethics of KVK community.
Course/GPPS adviser duties. The adviser must encourage the author of the course paper
or GPPS to find rational solutions on their own; therefore, the adviser does not provide the student
with ready-made solutions, but only coordinates the course paper/GPPS writing plan, based on the
given deadlines, controls the progress of the work, provides topical and methodological assistance,
and consultations.
Graduation paper adviser duties. The adviser must encourage the author of the graduation
paper to find rational solutions for the issue/topic in question. For this reason, advisers do not offer
ready-made solutions, but only assist in compiling the work plan, control the course of the work based
on the given schedules, offer topic and methodological assistance, suggestions for the improvement
of the paper, and references to consultants (if needed).
Reviewer duties. Reviewers must objectively identify the strengths and weaknesses of the
graduation paper and assess its compliance with the requirements specified in this methodology.

2. WRITING COURSE PAPERS, GRADUATION PAPERS FOR PEDAGOGICAL

2.1. Selecting the topic
Themes for course papers and GPPS are provided at the departments on the first week of
each semester. Students are free to choose the object and topic of their papers, based on the chosen
theme. The graduation paper of pedagogical studies examines topics in education sciences,
encompassing knowledge of theoretical pedagogy, applied pedagogy, psychology, and subject
didactics. The student compiles the plan (topic, objective, problem, tasks, content, research methods)
of the course paper, GPPS, or graduation paper, coordinates it with the adviser, and submits it to the
department no later than the second week of the semester, during which the paper is being written
(Annex 1). Academic departments certify course paper topics and the schedule for the research and
defence on the third week of the semester. The schedule is introduced to the course paper and GPPS
advisers and students.
The topics/directions of the graduations papers for the study programmes are announced on
the second week of September, each academic year. Students can freely choose the object and topic
of their final project, based on the theme chosen. Graduation paper topics, based on the
theme/directions announced, can be suggested by students, lecturers, social partners, etc. Graduation
papers, written, based on topics and themes, suggested by social partners, are regarded as
commissioned graduation papers. When a student completes a graduation paper to solve a problem
relevant to a specific organization, improve a process, etc., a representative of the organization fills
out a feedback form on the commissioned graduation paper (Annex12) and evaluates the applicability
of the submitted suggestions.
Based on the chosen theme, students formulate their graduation paper topic and objectives,
compile a graduation paper writing plan (Annex 2), and coordinate it with their adviser. The
coordinated graduation paper topic and plan must be submitted to the department on the last week of
November. The list of preliminary topics, compiled by the head of the department, is certified at the
department’s meeting. The lists of graduation paper topics and advisers are certified by the Dean of
the Faculty of Business on the second week after the end of the final internship. After that, graduation
paper topics and advisers can be changed at the suggestion of the head of the department only for
important reasons.
A single vocational bachelor’s graduation paper research can be conducted by two students
if the topic requires different fields of knowledge or abilities, or if the specifics of the topic requires
to collect a larger amount of data.

When choosing the topic, object and theme of the course paper, GPPS, or graduation paper,
students must objectively and realistically assess their capabilities to conduct empirical research, i.e.
answer the questions: is there an organization in which the object of the research can be identified; is
it possible to conduct such an empirical study in this organization (will the head of the organization
allow the study to be conducted); will the collected data be reliable (will the number of respondents
be sufficient, etc.), etc.
When writing course papers, GPPS, or graduation papers, it is necessary to obtain a
permission from the head of the organization regarding the use of the organization’s name and data
(Annex 3). Upon obtaining such a permission, students/graduates of pedagogical studies conduct their
research at pedagogical institutions (Annex 4). Upon obtaining a permission, the name of such
organisation is featured in the title, content, and text of the course paper, GPPS, or graduation paper.
In the event that the head of the organization does not allow the use of the name (but allows to conduct
of the study and use the data) (Annex 5), the organization in which the study is conducted is
mentioned in general or by indicating the field of its activity: a trading company, an institution, a
hotel, a vocational school, a factory, a warehouse, a transport company, a forwarding company, a
shop, a division of a manufacturing company, a department of a company, etc. The title cannot feature
indications like Company X or Institution Y. If the permission regarding the use of the name was not
obtained, only the permission to conduct a research must be submitted to the department. Permissions
are provided in the course paper, GPPS, or graduation paper as Annexes.
A permission to use the organisation’s data is not required if is publicly available (e.g. budget
data of the Republic of Lithuania, data from Statistics Lithuania, etc.).

Course papers may be both theoretical and practical.
In theoretical course papers, students present an analysis of various scientific works on the
issue in question, systematising and summarising various theories. The issue is solved from a
theoretical point of view by analysing scientific and didactic literature, and a theoretical solution to
the problem is presented by analysing the opinions, theories and research, conducted on the issue in
question by various scientists. The amount of literature reviewed in these course papers review must
be larger (at least 20 sources) than in practical course papers. The summary of the literature sources
in a theoretical course paper must correspond to the purpose and objectives of the paper. It is also
recommended to compare the solution for the issue with the works, written by Lithuanian and foreign
researchers.
In case of practical course papers, students present a theoretical analysis of the topic in
question, analyse at least 15 literary sources to substantiate the interpretation of the empirical data
obtained from them, and the results of an independently conducted empirical study. An empirical
study can be either quantitative, or qualitative.
2.3. Title page, content
The title page of a course paper or a GPPS must contain the names of the university, faculty,
and department; the title of the paper, the words course paper or graduation paper of pedagogical
studies, the student’s name, surname and signature, the position, academic title, name, surname, and
signature of the course paper or GPPS adviser, and the place and year of writing the course paper or
GPPS (Annex 6). The title page of a graduation paper must feature the names of the university,
faculty and department; the title of the paper, the type of the paper and the state code of the study
programme, the title of the field of study, the student’s name, surname and signature, the position,
academic degree, name, surname and signature of the adviser, the place and year of writing the
graduation paper (Annex 7). The title pages must be written in font 12. Titles of the faculty and
department, title of the course paper or a GPPS, or a graduation paper must be written in bold capital
letters.
The title page of the paper is followed by the table of contents, indicating titles of the
sections and subsections of the course paper, GPPS, or a graduation paper, and their page numbers
(Annex 8). The table of contents itself is not included. The table of contents is compiled using the

--- PDF page 14 ---
automatic content function in Times New Roman 12 font.
Requirements for the table of contents of course papers, GPPS, and graduation papers.
▪ The main parts of the content – theoretical analysis, overview of research methodology,
practical part – are written in capital letters, while their subsections and other parts of the paper – in
lowercase, font size 12. The annexes, listed in the table of contents are provided in font size 10.
▪ Theoretical and practical parts (sections) must be divided into subsections (e.g., 2.1.; 2.2.,
etc.). Subsections, if necessary, can also be divided into smaller sections (e.g., 3.1.1.; 3.1.2., etc.). A
section must have at least 2 subsections.
▪ Each numbered subsection of the theoretical and practical parts of the paper must have
its own structure – introductory sentences, the main part and a summary; therefore a numbered part
of the text of less than one page is insufficient. These parts must be evenly interconnected in
implementing the goal and objectives of the course paper, a GPPS, or a graduation paper. Results
should be described in a scientific style, and larger quantities of data should be presented in tables
and graphs.
▪ If a student receives permission from a company manager to use the company name in
their graduation paper, this must be indicated in the content. In cases, when the consent is not
received, the organization of the research object is mentioned in the content in general or by indicating
the field of activity: a trading company, an institution, a hotel, a vocational school, a factory, a
warehouse, a transport company, a forwarding company, a shop, a division of a manufacturing
company, a company department, etc. The title cannot feature indications like Company X or
Institution Y.
▪ The content of the graduation paper must reveal that the student analyses the same aspects
both in the theoretical and practical parts.
▪ The wording of the titles of theoretical and practical sections and subsections must be
original, created by the author himself, and not copied from literary sources.
▪ Titles of the subsections of the practical part must correspond to the wording of the work
tasks. If the task raised was to evaluate something, then the title of the appropriate subsection would
likely end with the word assessment, etc. The title should preferably focus on what was done by the
author: ...analysis, ...improvement, ...establishment, ...opportunities, etc. Otherwise, there might be
an impression that the author is writing a report and does not present his personal opinion on the topic
being analysed.
▪ The list of tables, figures, glossary, summary, introduction, conclusions and suggestions,
bibliography, and list of annexes are not numbered (no numbers are written next to their titles).
▪ The content is generated in font 12; the list of annexes – in font 10.

2.10.2.1. Quantitative research methods and quantitative research sample formation
They are referred to as quantitative methods because their goal is to obtain quantitative
information about a large number of objects (consumers, companies, or suppliers). Such studies are
conducted when it is necessary to obtain accurate, statistically reliable numerical data. The traditional
empirical quantitative research method is a survey. Its goal is to draw conclusions about the behaviour
of the entire population from a small part (sample) of the population (Bilevičienė and Jonušauskas
2011; Bryman, 2016).
The following are the quantitative methods most commonly used in university student papers
and the essential aspects of selecting the sample.
2.10.2.1.1. Written survey
According to Kardelis (2017), a written survey is the most popular method of sociological
research. A written survey enables to survey groups of various sizes – from several dozen to several
hundred people – in a short period of time. The essential feature of a written survey is that respondents
answer the questions in writing (Kara, 2015). A written survey is a structured procedure for

--- PDF page 26 ---
interviewing a respondent, usually carried out using printed questionnaires that are distributed to large
study groups. A distinctive feature of this method is that the questionnaire is completed by the
respondent independently, without the intervention of a surveyor (Gaižauskaitė, Mikėnė, 2014).
Therefore, a written survey is usually larger in scope and less personal in nature.
Preparation for a written survey. First, it is necessary to define, who will take part in the
research, and the limits of competence of the subjects. This information is used to create the
questionnaire. A questionnaire is a formalized set of questions to obtain information from
respondents (Dikčius, 2011). It is compiled based on the research objective, tasks, and research
questions – indicators. According to Dikčius (2011, p. 3), the main task of a questionnaire is to ensure
proper communication between the surveyor and the respondent. This questionnaire assignment
revolves around three major aspects: first, it must contain specific questions that the respondent will
be able and will have to answer. Both the questions and the possible answers provided ensure the
ability to later compare different people’s answers to the same question. Second, the questionnaire
must motivate and encourage the respondent’s involvement in the research, cooperation, and
participation until the end of the survey. Lack of order in the questionnaire cause additional problems
for the respondent in answering the questions, and at some point the respondent may refuse to
continue participating in the survey. Unfortunately, information obtained during an incomplete
survey usually cannot be used in data analysis, but obtaining that information required certain time
and financial resources. Third, you must reduce response errors. They occur when a respondent
provides inaccurate answers. Sometimes such answers may be deliberate, but it often happens that
the question itself or even individual words of it lead the respondent towards the wrong answer. This
leads to the provision of false information. To reduce these errors, the questionnaire needs to be well
planned.
General requirements for the questionnaire (Valackienė, Mikėnė, 2008; Kardelis, 2017):
1. First, there must be a motivated, logical explanation of who is conducting the research,
why the research is being conducted, its purpose, and the procedure for using the results. Emphasize
the importance and significance of the respondent’s personal participation in the survey, and their
open and fair responses. Guarantee the anonymity of the survey. Also, provide a short instruction on
filling out the questionnaire.
2. The respondent’s effort to answer must be minimal, so the questions must be specific
with understandable answer options.
3. The less the respondent has to write, the more they trust that anonymity will be
maintained.
4. The size of the questionnaire is important: a long questionnaire discourages the
respondent, they get tired of reading it carefully resulting in superficial answers; the design of the

--- PDF page 27 ---
questionnaire and the composition of the questions are also important – this can mitigate any negative
attitudes that may arise.
5. The initial questions are designed to capture the interest and engage respondents in
communication, gradually involving them in the work. The purpose of subsequent questions is to
obtain factual, event-based information known to the respondent. You should avoid questions that
require only one answer. The questionnaire should end with easy questions that summarize the topic
under consideration. Do not give questions that are too complex, difficult to understand, or may make
the respondent nervous.
Recommendations for formulating questions (Kardelis, 2017):
1. Content of the questions: is the question necessary? What makes it useful? Do you need
several questions or just the one? Does the respondent have the necessary information to answer the
question? Will we get the information we ask for?
2. Wording of the questions: can the question be misinterpreted? Will the incorrect
wording or emotional tone of the question make the answer unacceptable? Would an impersonal
question be more effective?
3. Placement of questions in a questionnaire: will the previous question affect the answer
to the following question? For example, if we ask a respondent, when they read fiction and then about
their free time, the respondent may indicate reading as one of their favourite free time activities; in
this case, we should ask about free time first and only then about reading. Is the sequence of questions
good from a psychological point of view? Biographical data that is not interesting to the respondent
should be provided at the end of the questionnaire. More intimate questions should also be postponed
until the end, and a series of identical questions should include an occasional different one. Questions
should go from simpler to more complex. Thus, ask the easier questions at the beginning.
Types of questions (Valackienė, Mikėnė, 2008):
1. Open-ended questions, i.e. questions that do not provide answer options. Advantages:
the easiest way for the researcher to formulate a question with a high probability that the answer truly
reflects the opinion of the subject. Disadvantages: respondents usually avoid or do not answer these
questions at all, the answers received are difficult to record, categorize and code, and it requires a
very high level of researcher competence to accurately record and interpret the thoughts expressed by
respondents.
2. Closed questions that have multiple-choice answers. Advantages: respondents can
quickly answer such questions because they are convenient and simple, data can be easily recorded
and analysed, and misunderstandings can be avoided more easily. Disadvantages: it is difficult to
anticipate all the answer options, and particularly difficult to formulate the question in a way that it
is properly understood and the answer options are clear.

--- PDF page 28 ---
3. Semi-closed questions have a certain number of answer options and a line, where you
can enter your own answer option if none of the provided answer options are suitable. Advantages:
similar to closed questions. Advantages: similar to open-ended questions.
Conducting a written survey. The respondent completes the questionnaire independently,
and can choose the speed of completion that is right for them, as well as a convenient time and place.
There is also more privacy with no discomfort that may arise when answering some (especially
sensitive or personal) questions directly to the researcher. Respondents feel like they can answer the
questions more openly and honestly. When completing the questionnaire themselves, respondents can
reread an unclear or difficult question, avoiding the potential inconvenience of having to ask the
interviewer to repeat the question (Ornstein, 2013, Gaižauskaitė, Mikėnė, 2014). On the other hand,
when only the questionnaire filling process involves the respondent alone, it is important to make
sure that they can understand the questions; therefore the questionnaire must have a sufficiently clear
structure, the questions must be easy to understand, and complex filtering steps should be avoided.
In addition, completing questionnaires independently leaves an opportunity to skip questions, leave
the questionnaire incomplete, which is particularly relevant to open-ended questions. Also, if the
questionnaire instructions are not clear enough, there may be more incorrectly answered questions.
Data analysis and interpretation. Kardelis (2002, 2017) highlights several tips on proper
analysis and interpretation of data collected through written surveys: 1) It is customary to use the
impersonal grammatical form to describe the results obtained. For example, instead of writing ‘the
results obtained in my research...’, write ‘the results of the research...’; 2) there are two main methods
of graphical data presentation: tables and figures (graphs, diagrams, etc.). Data that is examined using
logical analysis is presented in tables, while an entire phenomenon or development trends are better
illustrated using visuals; 3) scientific ethics using tables or figures published by other authors without
indicating their source; 4) illustrative material and descriptive text must complement, not duplicate
each other. Thus, listing all numbers, indicated in a table is has no purpose. The task of a descriptive
text is to direct the reader towards the principles of creating visual material, based on characteristic
indicators; 5) it is important to refrain from overloading the text with too many tables or pictures, as
this makes it difficult to read.

For example, some questions can be presented in a visual form (e.g., videos, etc.); the questionnaire
can be designed in a way that respondents with visual impairments can have the questions read aloud
or use the option of increasing the font size, etc. (Gaižauskaitė, Mikėnė, 2014).
Surveys online offer a greater chance that the respondent will provide a true and honest
answer. Moreover, these answers will be more open than in cases, when the survey takes place face-
to-face. That is because, in an online survey, respondents feel safe realising that no one knows the
answer options but themselves, etc. (Cornell, 2022)
Online research has a prerequisite – sufficient technological literacy of the researcher and
research participants, because not all people are able to use technological tools.
During the preparation phase, the researcher must remember three main questions: How to
get the respondent’s attention? What can be offered to the respondent? What platform is best for
conducting an online survey?
Choosing the right online platform is important not only for the conduct of the research and
its success, but also for data processing. The major platforms that students most often use are Google
Forms, www.apklausa.lt, Zohosurvey, SurveyNuts, Surveymonkey, Surveyplanet, Jotform, etc.
Conducting an online survey guarantees access to data worldwide, minimal time
investment of time and resources, and different tools for collecting information (e.g., e-mail, QR code,
embedded systems/websites, etc.). The recommendations for preparing questions for an online survey
(Kardelis, 2017) are the same as for a written survey (see pp. 20–23).
2.10.2.2. Qualitative research methods
Qualitative research includes very different research methodologies, which are divided into
several large groups: focus groups; in-depth (personal) interviews; semi-structured (formalized)
interviews; observations; projective methods; associative methods; document content analysis; case
studies; expert evaluation, etc. (Bilevičienė and Jonušauskas, 2011; Bryman, 2016; Žydžiūnaitė and
Sabaliauskas, 2017). Qualitative methods examine words, images, and imagined (subjective) things.
Most often, qualitative research is the beginning of quantitative research. Qualitative research is
limited to a small number of informants, so the results of the research method usually cannot be
generalized to the entire population. Qualitative research provides a lot of valuable information that
helps clarify and precisely define problems, which is why it is used by many researchers.
Further below is general information about the most commonly used qualitative research
methods.

2.10.2.2.1. Structured and semi-structured interview
In order to get a better understanding of a phenomenon, process or object under study,
information can be collected through interviews. An interview is usually defined as a conversation
between a researcher and a subject (informant) with the purpose to collect information necessary for
the study, and the conversation takes place by asking and answering questions. An interview is a way
to gather knowledge about various phenomena or learn about personal experience (Gaižauskaitė,
Valavičienė, 2016). The advantage of this type of communication is that subjects express their
thoughts and emotions naturally and spontaneously. The researcher focuses on the personal
experiences of the study participants in order to understand how people perceive and make sense of
their lives. However, the researcher must remember that the interview also involves the assessment
of the interaction between the researcher and the research participants, and how the subjects respond
to the questions asked, ensuring that they feel comfortable (Žydžiūnaitė, Sabaliauskas, 2017).
The interview is based on open-ended questions, expecting to receive the broadest, most
detailed, and most open answers possible, formulated and presented by the research participants
(informants), reflecting on their perspective. An interview involves an in-depth exploration of
opinions, attitudes, experiences, motives, feelings, etc. The researcher must ask questions in a way
that encourages the research participant to speak, present their point of view, and open up
(Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė, 2016, p. 80). The literature mentions a wide variety of interview methods
most commonly used in peer studies (Žydžiūnaitė, 2006, p. 46, Brunt et. al., 2017, p. 151):
1. A structured interview, where the subject is interviewed using a prepared questionnaire,
strictly following the order of the questions (Žydžiūnaitė, 2006, p. 46).
2. A focused or semi-structured interview, which has a plan, highlighting only the main
aspects, but the order of the questions is not determined (Žydžiūnaitė, 2006, p. 46).
Students, who are just beginning to gain experience as researchers, are recommended to
choose a structured or semi-structured interview method. The general steps of applying the interview
method are provided below.
Preparation for an interview. The researcher preparing for an interview must compile a
questionnaire consisting of open-ended questions (without providing the subjects with answer
options). The questions must cover the entire subject of the study. A structured open-ended
questionnaire does not deviate from the purpose and direction of the research, maintaining a certain
order, and research participants answer clear, specific questions, which makes it easier to sort,
compare, and analyse the information when processing data and preparing a research report. The
introductory questions in the questionnaire are usually broad and general, designed to start a
conversation and somewhat touch on the topic of the interview. Gaižauskaitė and Velavičienė (2016)
mention question formation strategies like thematic blocks and subsections, recommending to give

--- PDF page 31 ---
the more general questions first, and then follow with questions that are more specific and detailed.
Preferably, the research questions should correspond to the theoretical part of the student’s work, i.e.,
there should be questions from all subsections of the theoretical part. Questions may be about the
experiences, behaviours, places, environments, formal and informal roles, relationships, opinions,
values, feelings, etc. of the individuals participating in the study. The end of the interview
questionnaire often includes a question whether the research participant would like to add anything
to their story that was not asked about, but that they think is important. Literature recommends to
refrain from questions that are interrogative and lead to short answers, using questions that encourage
the sharing of information instead, e.g. start with how, what, when, who, or asking to tell, indicate,
convey an experience, etc.
Selecting research participants (informants). Qualitative research and interviews involve
research searching for participants (informants), whose personal experience, characteristics, and
social contexts would enable them to answer the research questions in the most accurate and
comprehensive way. Moreover, such research needs participants whose special, specific
characteristics would enable them to best reflect and/or inform about the phenomenon studied
(Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė, 2016, p. 37). The goal of the research defines the most appropriate and
informative target population (who will be able to provide the most necessary and/or essential
information about the phenomenon studied, and whether there are different subgroups (group
diversity) that can provide different opinions about the phenomenon under study). It is also necessary
to select participants, who represent a variety of positions related to the research subject and best
reflect possible significantly different experiences. More details on selecting the informants and
research participants are provided in the publication by Gaižauskaitė and Valavičienė (2016, pp. 36–
44). The research sample (the total number and composition of interviewees) can be finalized during
the processes of data collection and analysis.
Research scope (number of research participants). Without experience of conducting
interviews, it is recommended to make the decision of the sample size in two stages: at the beginning
of the study, consider and justify the initial expected number of study participants (for example, five
or six interviews), and after conducting these interviews, decide whether the collected data is truly
sufficient to answer the research questions. If the data collected does not seem to be sufficient, make
the decision on how many additional interviews are needed.
Data collection/interview. When planning an interview, it is necessary to assess and discuss
how much time the interview would take with the research participants in advance. The length of
interviews is directly related to the topic and number of questions, the researcher’s ability to interview
the subjects, and if the subjects are talkative or not. However, most interviews last 0.5–1.5 hours. An
interview can be conducted directly between the researcher and the subject or using information and

--- PDF page 32 ---
communication tools (telephone conversation, remote communication tools (MS Teams, Google
Meet, Messenger, etc.). Comfort, privacy, silence, and peace are among the most important
characteristics of an interview environment. Most often, an interview conversation is recorded using
various audio recording devices (e.g., telephone, recorder, specialized applications). Thus textual
information is fully and accurately recorded, making it easier for the researcher to control the
conversation and note non-verbal information (facial expressions, gestures, changes in voice tone,
etc.), as this is just as useful information as words. Video recording is more typical for specific
discussions and is rarely used for individual interviews, as it disturbs the research participant too
much (Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė, 2016, p. 108). In addition, the interviewee must be informed about
the recording of the interview in advance, explaining the purpose of the recording (for example, to
ensure the accuracy of the information), and obtaining research participant’s consent to make an audio
and/or video recording.
At the beginning of the interview, the researcher must introduce himself, explain the purpose,
inform about the recording, ask the research participant to tell about the essential social/professional
characteristics of the research participant, which would reveal his awareness of the topic, etc. The
researcher must be able to maintain a conversation, reformulate or clarify the question, and encourage
the research participant to open up. The researcher must also be prepared or have thought about
unexpected situations, for example, if the research participant/informant becomes agitated, starts
crying, reacts suspiciously to questions, etc. While listening to the response, the researcher must
interpret whether he or she understands what the research participant wants to say – if not, ask one or
more clarifying questions, ask the informant to explain the meaning of the words or expressions used;
reflect on whether the content of the response really includes what the researcher wants to know – if
not, it is necessary to immediately decide what additional questions could be asked so that the
informant understands the purpose of this interview. During actual interviews, research participants
often respond in unexpected ways – their answers are too short, to narrow, or they do not understand
the question. In such cases, it is appropriate to ask the research participant to provide an example or
a more detailed explanation. Also, during the interview, the researcher must observe the research
participant’s behaviour, non-verbal language, and the interview environment.
Preparing the interview data for analysis. The information collected during interviews is
processed by making verbatim interview transcripts, which are presented in the interview protocols
as work annexes (see Annex 10). These annexes are numbered and their number must represent the
number of the research informants. While making a transcript, it is necessary to neutralise the data of
the research participants to ensure their anonymity. Research participants/informants are coded or
numbered, for example, the first research participant is coded as D1, the second – D2, etc. Transcripts
include demographic, social or professional characteristics of the research participants; location,

--- PDF page 33 ---
date/time, duration; the researcher’s questions and the research participant’s answers are presented
sequentially according to the interview. The audio recording of the interview is converted into a
written word-for-word text. Transcripts enable us to ensure correct application of the method, correct
citing, thorough analysis, and correct interpretation. At this stage, it can be decided whether the
information collected is sufficient for data analysis and formulation of the results. If the informants,
who participated in the study were silent and did not provide sufficient information, it may be
necessary to seek for additional respondents.
Data analysis and interpretation. The validity and meaningfulness of the results of a
qualitative study is directly related to the richness of the information provided by the case selected.
Qualitative data analysis is the systematic arrangement of collected information and the search for
connections (Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė, 2016, p. 315). In scientific literature, data analysis and
interpretation is divided into the stages of data decomposition, data integration, and data
interpretation, as well as drawing conclusions (for more information, see Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė,
2016, p. 343). Qualitative data analysis means structuring textual information collected during
research, interpreting it, and searching for causal relationships and continuities. When structuring
qualitative data, interview excerpts and respondent statements should be presented in tables of
categories, subcategories, and supporting statements (see Annex11). The analysis involves
interpreting what the research participants said, searching for certain patterns by comparing what was
said in one interview with what was said in another, and by linking the information said by different
research participants.
2.10.2.2.2. Focus group
Focus group method is classified as a group interview method that is used to obtain
qualitative data when conducting qualitative research (Rupšienė, 2007; Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė,
2016). The focus group method is explained as (Rupšienė, 2007; Bitinas, Rupšienė, Žydžiūnaitė,
2008):
1. a qualitative research format for collecting detailed descriptive data in small groups, the
participants of which agree to focus on a topic of interest to all present;
2. a type of in-depth interview that involves an interaction in a small group (6–12) of
people, who share the same characteristics and who answer questions posed by other
group members and ask their own;
3. a form of a group interview, when there is interaction between group participants, which
becomes evident when discussing the research question;
4. a form of research that emphasizes the experiences, interests, attitudes, perspectives, and
opinions of group participants.

--- PDF page 34 ---
According to Bitinas, Rupšienė, Žydžiūnaitė (2008, p. 169), the distinctive features of the
group discussion method are as follows:
1. a small number of group participants (usually 8–12 people);
2. the group work is led by a moderator, who encourages discussion among group members;
3. each group participant is encouraged to express their opinion on the research question,
ask questions and answer questions from other group members;
4. group participants are selected based on a common characteristic (sociodemographic
background, experience, interests, etc.), therefore, they have common experiences or are
connected by common interests;
5. during the interview, the discussion of the problematic issue is directed in a way that
helps the researcher understand what is happening.
The group discussion method includes elements of an interview and observation. However,
the most important element of the method is the interview, where the added value lies in the active
interaction and relationship of the research participants (i.e. opinions, attitudes, approaches,
experiences, evaluations, beliefs, etc.) with the topic researched (Rupšienė, 2007; Bitinas, Rupšienė,
Žydžiūnaitė, 2008; Gaižauskaitė, 2012; Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė, 2016).
Preparing to moderate a focus group. According to Bitinas, Rupšienė, Žydžiūnaitė (2008,
p. 175), the person moderating the group discussion is referred to as a moderator. This could also be
the researcher, who has the necessary competencies to moderate a focus group discussion. The
moderator is fully responsible for the application of this method. The work of the focus group,
engaged in a discussion, led by the moderator, is referred to as a session. However, it is necessary to
carry out some preparatory work before the session, which will determine the quality of the data
collected during the session. Since a focus group explores a specific topic, before starting the session,
the researcher should list all the questions that he or she believes are necessary to answer the main
research issue and that need to be explored in the group. The focus group discussion leader must
select the group participants and coordinate their participation (verbally, in writing). A few days
before and in the evening, the moderator reminds the group participants about the working hours they
have planned. The time and length must be coordinated with the group participants. The group’s
workplace is coordinated with the group participants and, if necessary, determined by the researcher
himself. Knowing the location where the group work will take place, the moderator must consider
the seating of the participants. Whatever furniture is in the room, it must be arranged so that the seated
group members can see each other’s faces. To ensure interaction between group participants, it is a
good idea to provide each group participant with a name card. The group moderator must consider
the entire course of the session, from the group participants’ welcome to farewell, the basic rules of
the group’s work, and the main elements of the work. The group moderator must plan the method of

--- PDF page 35 ---
recording the session data (using video and audio recording equipment or not) and coordinate this
with the group participants (Rupšienė, 2007).
Application of the focus group method. A discussion session usually lasts 1.5-2 hours, but
can be shorter depending on the objectives of the study. The main part of the group discussion/focus
group session is a discussion of the research questions. The moderator asks questions, clarifies, asks
additional questions, and asks for a more detailed explanation or justification of their opinion. During
this session, it is not enough to simply ask the questions; the moderator must also receive detailed
answers to these questions. Group participants rarely give detailed answers. Therefore, the moderator
must be able to talk the group participants into providing the desired information. Major questions
and statements: Why yes? Why not? Please give more detail! What did you do? What do you use?
Why didn’t you do/use something? etc. (Rupšienė, 2007; Bitinas, Rupšienė, Žydžiūnaitė, 2008).
According to Gaižauskaitė, Valavičienė (2016, p. 148), the information produced by group
discussions is both verbal and non-verbal, which supplements the data collected. In addition to
individual aspects of non-verbal language, it is also possible to observe elements of group
interaction: the development of the discussion and new questions among the participants, emergence
of other topics, resolution of disagreements on the issues under discussion, etc. Sometimes non-verbal
information not only significantly complements verbal information, but can also be much more
eloquent.
At the end of the group discussion, the moderator must immediately check if the discussion
data has been recorded. Then the moderator writes down their reflections and observations about the
session that took place (Rupšienė, 2007; Bitinas, Rupšienė, Žydžiūnaitė, 2008).
The preparation of the group discussion data for analysis, as well as the analysis and
interpretation of data is carried out in exactly the same way as in the case of an individual interview.
This was discussed in more detail in section 2.10.1 Structured and semi-structured interviews (see
pp. 24–26).
2.10.2.2.3. Case study
Researchers deciding to conduct a case study work in a natural environment, without
making any impact on the situation studied, but looks for explanations that help to better understand
their observations (Kardelis, 2017). By applying this method, the researcher delves into the subtleties
of the behaviour or activities of people or institutions, the analysis of complex relationships, and the
expression of the impact of unforeseen factors, i.e. the characteristics of the object (Bitinas, Rupšienė
& Žydžiūnaitė, 2008).
What makes the case study method stand out as an independent form of research from others
is not the methods, but the focus on the individual, single case. This case can be a person, a group, an

--- PDF page 36 ---
episode, a process, a community, a society, or any other element of social life. The researcher collects
all relevant and available data (Visuotinė Lietuvių enciklopedija, 2023).
A case study is usually conducted to achieve one of three goals: to provide a vivid description
(to clarify contexts, meanings, intentions), to provide explanations (the researcher needs to learn why
the situation is the way it is or why the individual behaves in a certain way), and to provide evaluations
(to evaluate the environment, programme, method, etc.) (Charles, 1999).
Data collection. Researchers using the case study method must collect data from multiple
sources in order to analyse the case under study as a whole. Some researchers recommend combining
quantitative and qualitative data collection methods, believing them to be equally useful, while others
suggest using only qualitative data exclusively. Researchers may apply testing, written and oral
interviews, and document analysis, but the bulk of data is collected through direct observation by the
researchers themselves (Yazan, 2015). This approach is also followed by Hamilton (2011), who states
that a case study is used to create a holistic picture of the research object, using various data collection
methods, as they can provide the researcher with detailed insights into the experiences of participants
in a specific context.
Data analysis methods. A typical feature of a comprehensive case study is that it enables
to collect a considerable amount of data, expressed in words, images, objects, and sometimes
numbers. There are three types of data analysis using a single case study method: interpretive (the
aim is to explain a phenomenon), structural (the aim is to find recurring patterns), and reflective (the
aim is to describe or evaluate a phenomenon based on intuition and imagination; this type of analysis
is subjective and does not have standard procedures). The data of a single case study method is
analysed in several stages: highlighting themes, that is, recurring characteristic features, grouping
them into categories, using these categories to create models, and then interpreting those models in
order to answer research questions or test the hypotheses raised (Visuotinė lietuvių enciklopedija,
2023).
2.10.2.2.4. Document content analysis
Document analysis method refers to primary data collection, when documents are used as
primary sources of information. Documents differ from each other in origin, availability, etc. Some
documents are created by state institutions, others – by private structures and business enterprises.
Text and audio recordings are stored by many agencies, making them available to researchers and
referred to as public official documents. Document analysis may also involve documents that are not
public, such as school records, social work records, health examination records, social work records,
police records, court records, and others. Commercial companies keep records of decisions made and
financial transactions. Such material must be kept confidential or made available in a form that keeps

--- PDF page 37 ---
individuals confidential. Document analysis requires adherence to certain principles. First, selecting
sources of information and defining the scope of the selection. The selection of the documents must
ensure that the information is reliable and valid. When checking reliability, it is necessary to perform
an external (conditions of creating the document) and internal (document content) analysis (Tidikis,
2003).
The effectiveness of using documents in a research may depend on the specific documents
used and how they are used. Documents can provide useful information about the culture of the
organization and the people working in it, project implementation, and personal attitudes and values
of the subjects. Documents can be useful as:
▪ the main data collection method (may be supplemented by other data collection methods,
such as interviews or observation);
▪ an additional data collection method (for example, in the preparatory interview stage,
when developing a questionnaire, or in the preparatory observation stage, when deciding
what to observe);
▪ as one of the data collection methods (Rupšienė, 2007).
The researcher describes why the specified data collection strategy was chosen and what
means were used to achieve data diversity. Data analysis is conducted with documents that would
ensure the reliability and validity of the information.
In the case of document analysis, according to Rupšienė (2007), there are no clear rules for
determining the sample size. The research sample can be quantitative (for example, 118 CMR
consignment notes of one month from one logistics organization) and qualitative (for example, one
CMR consignment note of one logistics organization and the information contained therein, and
information provided in the company’s balance sheet). In this section, it is necessary to describe the
criteria used to select the texts for the analysis. Also, define and justify the sample size of the study,
for example, indicate which documents would be used for data collection and why.
Data collection. Document analysis requires adherence to certain principles; therefore, first,
we need to select the sources of information and define the scope of the selection. The selection of
the documents must ensure that the information is reliable and valid (Tidikis, 2003). The data is
collected from written, printed or electronic documents relevant to the study – legal acts, operational
documents, financial reports. Only the data relevant to the research must be collected.
Data analysis method. Document analysis is conducted using classical (qualitative) and
content analysis methods (Kudinovienė, 2008). The classical analysis method involves obtaining
qualitative information on the basis of a large sample. This is an analytical review that presents
systematized, logically structured results of the research conducted, illustrated with tables, diagrams,
data maps, and other visual aids. Data analysis includes an assessment of the reasons for changes in

--- PDF page 38 ---
various events, indices, and their interrelations, and future predictions. If the data was analysed using
computer software (Kokybis, NVivo, Text Analysis Suite etc.) it should be mentioned as well. The
content analysis method is a quantitative analysis of social information. In the most general sense,
content analysis is a technique that enables to draw reliable conclusions after objectively and
systematically examining the features of a text. The essence of this method is to isolate certain
semantic units in the text of a document, then calculate the frequency of their use, and study the
relationships of various text elements both with each other and with the entire volume of information
(Tidikis, 2003).
2.10.2.2.5. Observation
Observation is regarded as: 1) direct sensory perception of actual events; 2) a method of
collecting primary social information, when research data is collected by the researcher purposefully
recording observed events, phenomena and processes occurring in natural conditions. This is a
particularly valuable method of collecting research data: applied purposefully and systematically, it
enables to obtain data about events, behaviour, and processes occurring in the natural environment
that do not change or influence the course of these events, i.e., to see reality as it is (Tidikis, 2003).
Scientific observation differs from non-scientific observation in that:
▪ it has a clear research goal and strictly formulated objectives;
▪ it is planned according to a model formed in theoretical analysis;
▪ it records observation data in protocols according to a certain system;
▪ the information thus obtained is controlled, reasonable and stable;
▪ the data is recorded using not only direct observation, but also technical measures;
▪ it has a hypothesis of the aspect studied, which is confirmed or refuted by the facts
obtained;
▪ the observer has mastered the observation technique and has accumulated personal
experience (Tidikis, 2003).
Observations can be included (covert), excluded (open), introspective, standardized, non-
standardized, partially standardized, field, laboratory, laboratory-field, systematic, random, episodic,
using technical data recording tools, without using technical data recording tools (Kardelis, 2017,
Tidikis, 2003, Adomaitienė, Zubrickienė, 2019). Valackienė and Mikėnė (2010) identify structured
observation as a quantitative research method, and unstructured observation as a qualitative research
method.
The observation method is appropriate for cases where informants do not know the exact
answers (for example, preschool children) or the aim is to get to know the physical environment of
the problem studied, gain a better understanding of interpersonal relationships, examine details of

--- PDF page 39 ---
certain events (projects, programmes), and investigate non-verbal communication (Adomaitienė and
Zubrickienė, 2019).
The main requirements for observation are as follows: observation must have a specific goal;
the number of characteristics observed must be optimal and precisely defined; observation must take
place according to a pre-determined plan – where does the observation take place, when, how, what
is the object and how long would the observation take place; data obtained during observation must
be comparable. Uniform evaluation criteria should also be applied (Adomaitienė and Zubrickienė,
2019).
Research scope. One of the relevant methodological questions is how many observations
should be made? This is not as relevant if the researcher is a member of the group observed, but it is
important if the observer comes from the outside. There are no strict rules. The main recommendation
is to observe more than once and, if possible, to take a longer pause between observations (Rupšienė,
2007).
Data collection method – in case of observation, there must be an indication of what type
of observation was chosen (unstructured or semi-structured) and why; how the observation was
carried out – what days were chosen, what was the observation time (in hours) for each day, and why
this particular time period; what was the duration of the entire observation in days; the conditions,
environment, and premises the observation was carried out in; object of observation – a person, a
group of people, the work of a department, or human interactions and statements why this particular
object of observation was chosen; whether the observation was carried out in several stages, and what
was the content of each stage; in case there was a preparatory stage, then it should also be described
– what was done and why; also, a description of how the observation data was entered – separate
computer files or one (Žydžiūnaitė, 2011).
During an observation, data is recorded in an observation protocol (see Annex 12), an
observation matrix, or in reflection notes, which are submitted as annexes to the paper.
Observation data is recorded in an observation protocol, which consists of:
▪ descriptive notes: description of the physical environment, events, activities, brief
reconstruction of dialogues that took place, drawings, portraits of participants, etc.;
▪ reflection notes: the researcher’s personal thoughts, feelings experienced during
observation, insights for future data coding, analysis, interpretation of results, ideas for
improving the course of the research, etc.;
▪ information relevant to the study may also be recorded: time, duration, location of a
specific observation, etc. (Morkevičius, Telešienė, Žvaliauskas, 2008).
An observation matrix means that a student, having analysed the theoretical material and
prepared the theoretical part of the paper, creates a table indicating specific criteria according to which

--- PDF page 40 ---
the observation will be carried out. In the table, each criterion must be detailed with indicators, and
the latter must be described during the observation. In this way, the researcher obtains focused
observation material and uses it to mark how many times a specific action, interaction, dialogue, etc.
occurred and repeated during a specific observation period. (Žydžiūnaitė, 2011).
Data analysis method. The most commonly used method is the content analysis. This is an
analytical review that presents systematized, logically structured results of the research conducted,
illustrated with tables, diagrams, data maps, and other visual aids.
2.10.2.2.6. Action research
Action research is designed to create new knowledge in order to find a specific solution to
a problem or discover a solution in a problematic situation. One of the distinctive features of action
research is the active and deliberate participation of the researcher in the research being conducted.
Unlike other empirical studies, in which the researcher is considered impartial towards the
participants, in action research the researcher is considered a key participant in the research process
and collaborates with other participants. This method is focused on the study and improvement of the
professional/social activities of the individual (who becomes the researcher). Action research is
usually undertaken by a person, who seeks to investigate their own activities. The most important
thing here for the researcher is to examine various aspects of the action and propose specific solutions
for its improvement (Adomaitienė and Zubrickienė, 2019). This is important for social sciences,
whose purpose is to suggest ways to improve the work with people or their groups (for example, for
management, education, social work, and nursing specialists). The researcher, while improving the
experience he/she already has, relies on the principle of social cognition: research by taking action,
and taking action while researching (Bitinas, Rupšienė and Žydžiūnaitė, 2008).
The application of action research in an educational institution is related to the professional
growth of the educator (Norton, 2009; Efrat, Ravid, 2013). The object of research is certain problems
in a specific environment (e.g. pedagogical). For example, in a pedagogical environment, action
research can investigate the opinions of learners, the development of certain skills, the application of
different teaching methods and/or application of methods at different times of the activity or while
working with different groups of learners (for more information, see Efrat, Ravid, 2013).
Action research inherently focuses on activity, which is simultaneously a method of
information gathering and practical activity (Naujanienė et al., 2016). These studies are conducted in
a natural environment, e.g., in the educational process (in other words, to study the phenomenon itself
and its course), rather than in an isolated, artificially created environment (Adomaitienė and
Zubrickienė, 2019). During the research, the researcher actively participates in the process being
studied and carries out activities that result in significant changes in performance outcomes. The

--- PDF page 41 ---
researcher aims that these changes would be planned by the participants themselves, while the
researcher creates favourable conditions, encouraging the participants to delve deeper into their
activities and behaviour (Bitinas, Rupšienė and Žydžiūnaitė, 2008).
There are two main categories of action research: descriptive research, which aims to find
out what is happening in a given environment, and a research designed to test a hypothesis or a chosen
innovation, implemented by the researcher (Sagor, 2005).
Organising action research. Researchers indicate a different number of stages in the
implementation of performance research, from four (Sagor, 2005), five (Norton, 2009) to a detailed
six (Efrat and Ravid, 2013) stages. However, researchers applying action research typically go
through a cycle of research, action, and reflection phases (the so-called action/action research cycle),
consisting of four main elements: identifying the problem, action planning, execution, and evaluation
(see Fig. 1). Since during the cycle it usually becomes clear that various elements that were not taken
into account in the original action plan, can be improved as well, researchers move from one cycle to
the next. Thus emerges the so-called action research spiral, which illustrates the complex and iterative
nature of this approach (Góral, Jałocha, Mazurkiewicz and Zawadzki, 2021).

acknowledge and disclose their subjectivity and observe its potential impact on their data collection

Research data collection. After identifying the research object, the researcher must decide
on the method of collecting information. This can be done by applying qualitative and quantitative
research methods: questionnaire, observation, interview, focus group discussion, case analysis,
diaries, document analysis, etc. This can be achieved using various research instruments
(questionnaire, survey, interview, observation, diary writing, reflection, observation protocol, etc.).
The choice of the method depends on the chosen research object, issues, specific conditions, interests,
attitudes, etc. (Adomaitienė and Zubrickienė, 2019; Efrat and Ravid, 2013).
Data analysis. The results of the study are presented taking into account the research
methods – qualitative or quantitative data analysis – used in conducting the action study.
2.11. Determining the research sample and identifying the principles of research ethics
A detailed description of the research methods used is then followed by the information
about the general population and the research sample. One of the necessary aspects of planning a
research is defining the minimum sample in order to formulate conclusions that are statistically
significant. The method of selecting an appropriate sample with the aim of identifying traits or
characteristics common to the entire population is called sampling. Sampling is necessary because,
due to financial and technical possibilities, rational calculation and logical justification, it is
impractical to interview or study all elements of the population or general set of interest.
Samples are drawn in various ways, but there are two main sampling methods: probability
and non-probability. In probability sampling, the probability of each element being included in the
sample is known, while in non-probability sampling, the probability of selection is unknown.

2.12. Practical part
The practical part of the work must be a research in nature, i.e. not limited to a description
and presentation of research data, but rather a critical (evaluative) analysis of problems, research data,
conclusions and proposals that have practical and applied value. The conclusions and suggestions in
this part must be reasoned and based on the results of applied research and insights from theoretical
analysis. It is recommended to formulate your conclusions and suggestions in a neutral grammatical
form, for example, ...determined, ...observed, ...suggested, ...proposed, ...recommended, etc. Abstract
suggestions ...to strive, ...to aim, ...to improve, ... a better, ...more are meaningless.

--- PDF page 47 ---
Tables and figures must be informative and neatly formatted. Each figure or table presented
in the text must be commented on and the information provided in them must be analysed. However,
it is not necessary to describe the content provided. If tables, graphs and other visual material
presented occupy an entire A4 page, this material must be presented in annexes.
The summaries of the subsections of this part must highlight the empirical research analysis
results. A correctly formulated summary shows whether the presented text answers the meaning of
the title of the section (subsection).
Introductory and summary paragraphs may contain sentences that connect one part of the
work to the previous or another part of the work. This creates the impression of continuity.
2.13. Conclusions and recommendations
The section of conclusions and recommendations present the results of the paper in a
summarized and concentrated manner. This should not be a copy and paste of the conclusions and
recommendations contained in the summaries of the analytical sections. Provide only the most
important statements, illustrating the result of each of the task implemented. That is why the
conclusions are numbered based on the research tasks. After presenting a conclusion that reveals the
problem, a proposal(s) is formulated to solve the problem. The feasibility of each of the suggestions
must be proved and reasoned in the practical part. Tasks, related to theory analysis are described in a
single conclusion.
This part should not exceed 1-2 pages.
2.14. Literature
The bibliography is compiled at the end of a course paper, GPPS, or graduation paper on a
new page in a separate section and under the heading Literature.
All sources must be provided in their original language. All sources of information in the
bibliography must be listed in alphabetical order. The bibliography indicates books, articles, and
documents that have been cited, discussed in one aspect or another, or commented on in the text.
Sources printed in Latin characters – Lithuanian, English, German, French, other languages – are
listed in alphabetical order first, followed by sources printed in Cyrillic or other characters.
Bibliographic descriptions of scientific publications and other publications used (cited, paraphrased
or mentioned) in the paper must also be arranged in alphabetical order according to international APA
rules or the recommendations of the LST ISO 690:2010 standard. It is recommended to have at least
one third of the literature resources by foreign authors, including half in a foreign language.

4. GRADUATION PAPER DEFENCE
4.1. Preparation for the defence of the graduation paper
For the public defence of the graduation papers, the director issues an order to form a
qualification commission with a chairman, based on recommendations of the dean of the faculty. The
commission must consist of at least 3 (5) members, unless the Description of the studies provides
otherwise. Graduation papers can be defended by students, who have completed all requirements,
established in the study programme.
The graduation paper review takes place at the department no later than 14 calendar days
prior the beginning of the public defence. It is attended by graduation paper advisers, students, and
the head of the department. The review can also be attended by other interested persons (lecturers,
students, social partners). The purpose of the graduation paper review is to review the work and listen
to the student’s presentation of the graduation paper, to assess whether all mandatory requirements
for the structure, content, scope and format of the final thesis have been met, and to give
recommendations of what should be corrected. The graduation paper advisers and the head of the
department review the graduation papers, offering recommendations to make the graduation paper
suitable for defence by the qualification committee.
Students must upload a properly written graduation paper on Moodle and agree to have the
graduation paper checked by the Turnitin plagiarism checking system 3 working days prior to the
review at the department. The author uploading the paper is responsible for its content and
authenticity. A graduation paper that has not been uploaded on Moodle and checked for plagiarism
and will not be evaluated or allowed to be submitted for review at the department.
Before the review of the graduation paper at the department, the graduation paper adviser
must become familiar with the graduation paper checking and verification report on Moodle and
Turnitin plugin. Before making a decision regarding the graduation paper review at the department,
the advisor must review the graduation final project plagiarism checker report. The total percentage
of overlap, which is considered plagiarism, must not exceed 20 percent (7 percent with a single
source). A paper may be considered unoriginal if the overall overlap with other sources (with and/or
without citing the source) accounts for more than 50 percent of the entire content. In such a case, the
graduation paper supervisor provides the head of the department with a decision to refuse to allow
the public defence of the graduation paper. Students are informed of the decision to allow or not to
allow access to public defence during the review at the department.
The content of graduation papers that were recognised by the department meeting as suitable
for public defence may be amended until the final submission for evaluation (public defence) at the

--- PDF page 58 ---
meeting of the qualification commission. Changes must be coordinated with the graduation paper
adviser.
Students must submit at least one printed copy of the graduation paper for public defence
and upload the electronic version to the eLABA system. The submission of the graduation paper is
registered in the graduation paper registration journal.
When the final version of the graduation paper is uploaded on the Turnitin peer review
system, the graduation paper adviser performs a peer review of the graduation paper once again and
approves it for defence. A graduation paper is allowed to be publicly defended if it does not contain
instances of text overlap that could be interpreted as plagiarism (the Turnitin system does not
determine whether a thesis is plagiarized; this can be determined only by the graduation paper adviser
based on the information on the detected overlaps provided). The graduation paper adviser must
submit written feedback on the graduation paper submitted (Annex 10) to the department no later
than 2 working days before the public defence. The graduation paper adviser offers their opinion on
the paper, but does not grade it.
The student has the right to become familiar with the questions raised by the graduation
paper reviewer at the department no later than one working day before the meeting of the graduation
paper evaluation committee.
Permission to defend the graduation paper is certified upon the recommendation of the dean
of the faculty by the order of the director no later than 6 working days before the meeting of the
graduation paper evaluation committee.

The graduation paper defence dates are announced no later than 30 calendar days until the
beginning of the public defence. The defence of the graduation paper takes place at the meeting of

The public meeting of the graduation paper defence qualification commission takes place in
Lithuanian language. In cases, when the graduation paper is submitted in English language or when
the participants of the meeting use English language, it may be translated into Lithuanian.

4.3. Evaluation
Graduation paper evaluation. The graduation paper is evaluated by a qualification reviewer
and a committee. The graduation paper is submitted to the department for a review no later than 5
working days before the meeting of the qualification commission. The exact date is determined by
the head of the department, where the student is defending the graduation paper. The reviewer must
objectively identify the strengths and weaknesses of the graduation paper, assess its compliance with
the formal requirements specified in this methodology, and present questions to the author, which he
or she will have to answer during the public defence. The reviewer evaluates the work on a ten-point
scale. The reviewer must submit the graduation paper evaluation (Annex 13) to the department no
later than two working days before the date of the qualification committee meeting. The student has
the right to become familiar with the evaluation of the graduation paper reviewer at the department
no later than one working day before the meeting of the qualification committee.
The graduation paper adviser’s comment (Annex 10) must be submitted to the department
no later than two working days before the public defence. The graduation paper adviser offers their
opinion on the paper, but does not grade it.
The qualification commission is formed each year by the order of KVK Director, upon the
recommendation of the Dean of the Faculty, to evaluate the study results achieved by students of
individual study programmes. The minimum number of commission members is 3 (5), unless the
study field description provides otherwise. The commission consists of one representative of the
organization, who is appointed as the chairman of the commission (whose qualifications correspond
to the field of study program), a scientist or a lecturer from another higher education institution, and
a lecturer from this study program. The composition of the qualification commission must comply
with the requirements of the study field descriptions.
The qualification commission is formed one month before the public defence.
The graduation papers are evaluated after the public defence at a closed meeting of the
qualification committee, which is recorded in minutes. Only members of the qualification
commission participate in the closed meeting and have the right to vote.
The graduation papers are evaluated unanimously on a ten-point grading scale.
Members of the qualification committee evaluate the graduation paper based on its
compliance with formal requirements, correct use of the Lithuanian language, demonstrated level of
achievement of the study programme results, and presentation of the graduation paper. The evaluation

--- PDF page 62 ---
of the graduation paper by the qualification commission is equal to the arithmetic average of the
evaluations of all commission members, rounded to a whole number. The final grade of the graduation
paper includes the reviewer’s evaluation, whose grade weighting coefficient is 0.2, and the evaluation
of the qualification commission, whose grade weighting coefficient is 0.8. In an event of a dispute
regarding the evaluation, the final decision is made by the chairman of the commission. The
graduation paper is considered defended if it receives an evaluation of at least 5 points.
The decision of the graduation paper qualification commission is approved in a closed
meeting by a protocol signed by the chairman of the qualification commission. The evaluation of the
graduation paper is recorded in the minutes of the qualification commission meeting and entered into
the final grade summary, which is used to prepare the higher education diploma supplement.
The decision on the evaluation of the graduation paper is made at a closed meeting of the

the Study Management Information System.
The decision of the qualification commission is final; appeals regarding the evaluation of
