## 1. Poverty, social exclusion and inequality: latest rates, EU ranking, most-affected groups

### Takeaway
Lithuania had the **highest at-risk-of-poverty (AROP) rate in the EU in EU-SILC 2025, at 22.6%**, up 1.1 pp from 21.5% in 2024. Its AROPE rate (risk of poverty or social exclusion) is among the EU's top five, at 25.8% in 2024. Poverty is concentrated among pensioners and older people (about 37–40% AROP), single parents, people with disabilities, the unemployed and the less educated. The EU and OECD both say the tax-benefit system does little to reduce poverty.

### Cited Findings
- **2024 (EU-SILC 2024, 2023 incomes):** AROP rate 21.5%, up 0.9 pp on 2023. Monthly poverty threshold EUR 616 for a single person and EUR 1,294 for two adults with two children under 14. — [State Data Agency (VDA), "Poverty indicators for 2024 have been presented"](https://vda.lrv.lt/en/news/poverty-indicators-for-2024-have-been-presented)
- The EU-average AROP in 2024 was 16.2%, well below Lithuania's. Child poverty reportedly reached 19%, up 2 pp. Attribution was uncertain in the search summary: the child figure appears to come from the EAPN report citing VDA. — [EAPN Poverty Watch Lithuania 2025 (published Mar 2026)](https://www.eapn.eu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/eapn-Poverty-Watch-Lithuania-2025-EN-6305.pdf); [VDA](https://vda.lrv.lt/en/news/poverty-indicators-for-2024-have-been-presented)
- **2025 (EU-SILC 2025):** A Statistics Lithuania headline states that the AROP rate rose by 1.1 pp in 2025. — [Statistics Lithuania news list](https://osp.stat.gov.lt/en/informaciniai-pranesimai?sritis=8). The 2025 level is 22.6% (previous 21.5%). — [Trading Economics, Eurostat data](https://tradingeconomics.com/lithuania/at-risk-of-poverty-rate-eurostat-data.html)
- **EU ranking 2025:** "According to EU-SILC 2025, Lithuania (22.6%), Latvia (22.0%), and Bulgaria (21.2%) recorded the highest at-risk-of-poverty rates." - **AROPE 2024:** 25.8%, tied with Spain as fifth-highest in the EU, behind Bulgaria (30.3%), Romania (27.9%) and Greece (26.9%). — [Format Research, citing Eurostat (Apr 2025)](https://formatresearch.com/en/2025/04/30/rischio-poverta-o-esclusione-sociale-nel-2024-eurostat/)

AROP for retired persons was 39.9% in 2024. — [Trading Economics / Eurostat](https://tradingeconomics.com/lithuania/at-risk-of-poverty-rate-retired-persons-eurostat-data.html)
- Older data point: pensioner poverty was 39.5% in 2020. — [EAPN Poverty Watch Lithuania 2020](https://eapn.eu/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/eapn-Poverty-Watch-Lithuania-Final-5259.pdf)
- On the OECD's measure (50% of median income), old-age relative income poverty in Lithuania is "20% or more". That is lower than Estonia and Latvia (both above 30%) but high by OECD standards. — [OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025, Old-age income poverty](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/11/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_76510fe4/full-report/old-age-income-poverty_3cd946d7.html)

- **European Commission (Social Convergence Framework, SWD(2025) 95, 11 Apr 2025):** Lithuania is one of ten Member States with "potential risks to upward social convergence". - Freedom House says "chronic problems including corruption and socioeconomic inequality often arouse public dissatisfaction" with government and institutions. — [Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025: Lithuania](https://freedomhouse.org/country/lithuania/freedom-world/2025)

### Inferences
- Poverty worsened in both 2024 (+0.9 pp) and 2025 (+1.1 pp), even though nominal wages, minimum wages and pensions were rising fast. The likely mechanism is relative: the threshold is 60% of median income, and median wage growth (driven by employees in Vilnius) outpaces pensions and benefits. Low-wage workers with full-time jobs are not the core group. The poor are mainly benefit and pension recipients, especially older people living alone. This reading is consistent with the EC's "low poverty reduction from social transfers" and the OECD's "tax-transfer system could do more".
- The nationals-only AROPE of 27.7% for 2025, against a total-population 25.8% for 2024, suggests AROPE also rose in 2025. Foreign nationals are a small share of Lithuania's population, so the two figures are close to comparable. This is an inference, not a published total-population figure.
- Unmet needs: (a) income top-ups or services aimed at single older people, mostly women, living alone; (b) single-parent support, including more reliable child-maintenance enforcement and childcare; (c) disability-related cost compensation. All three groups are named by the OECD and EC as most exposed, but this session found no 2025 group-specific evaluation data.

### Gaps
- No official **2025 Gini or S80/S20** figure was retrieved. The latest Gini found is the OECD's 0.366 for 2021 (old). The VDA/Statistics Lithuania 2025 release and Eurostat table ilc_di12 should be checked.
- The **total-population AROPE for 2025** and the 2025 euro-denominated poverty threshold were not found.
- No recent figures were retrieved for **single-person 65+ households, single-parent households or people with disabilities**. Historically these groups have AROP rates of roughly 50–60%+, but that could not be verified this session and should not be reported without a source.
- Rural vs urban poverty rates for 2024–2025 were not retrieved.
- The EAPN Poverty Watch Lithuania 2025 PDF could not be opened. It probably contains detailed group data and NGO recommendations.

## 2. Regional disparities: Vilnius vs the rest of Lithuania

### Takeaway
Wages, output and opportunity are concentrated in Vilnius. The capital region's average gross wage is about EUR 459/month higher than in Central and Western Lithuania. Vilnius city's average is roughly 1.6 times the lowest municipality's (Zarasai). Vilnius County produces about 45% of GDP with about 30% of the population, and OECD data show the gap between the richest and poorest regions has widened since 2011.

### Cited Findings

Capital Region average gross wage was EUR 2,592.10 (+1.3%) and CWL EUR 2,132.70 (−1%). The highest municipal average was Vilnius city at EUR 2,675.50 and the lowest Zarasai district at EUR 1,651.30. — [LRT, "Wage gap between Vilnius region and the rest of Lithuania widens"](https://www.lrt.lt/en/news-in-english/19/2580729/wage-gap-between-vilnius-region-and-the-rest-of-lithuania-widens). **Date caution:** the search summary labelled this Q4 2025, but the article's URL number places it around June 2025, so it probably covers an earlier quarter (likely Q1 2025). The EUR 459 figure appears for different quarters in different sources.
- Secondary source for Q1 2026: Vilnius city municipality EUR 2,891.40 gross and CWL region EUR 2,349.00. — [Finorum (aggregator)](https://finorum.com/average-salary-in-lithuania/)
- Vilnius County GDP per capita was about EUR 41,500 in 2024. Vilnius generates about 45% of national GDP, and the county holds about 30% of the population (872,685 residents in 2024). — [Wikipedia, Vilnius County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilnius_County); [Lithuania.lt, "Vilnius surpasses Baltic capitals and EU average"](https://lithuania.lt/governance-in-lithuania/vilnius-surpasses-baltic-capitals-and-eu-average-a-baltic-tiger-on-the-rise/) (secondary sources)
- Vilnius County GDP per capita was EUR 35,300 in 2022. — [Wikipedia, List of regions of Baltic States by GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_regions_of_Baltic_States_by_GDP)
- The Central and Western Lithuania region (the NUTS-2 region covering everything except Vilnius region) had GDP per capita of EUR 21,273 in 2023. — [Wikipedia, Central and Western Lithuania Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_and_Western_Lithuania_Region)
- OECD: the GDP-per-capita gap between the richest region (Vilnius) and the poorest (Tauragė) "has been slightly increasing since 2011". — [OECD Regions and Cities at a Glance 2018, Lithuania note](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/01/oecd-regions-and-cities-at-a-glance-2018-country-notes_a745da06/lithuania_24361556/673ec1cb-en.pdf). - Old data (2017): Utena had the highest unemployment rate, 10 pp higher than Vilnius. — [OECD Regions and Cities at a Glance 2018, Lithuania note](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/01/oecd-regions-and-cities-at-a-glance-2018-country-notes_a745da06/lithuania_24361556/673ec1cb-en.pdf)

### Inferences
- Average gross pay in Zarasai (EUR 1,651) is about 62% of Vilnius city's (EUR 2,676), a gap of about EUR 1,024/month. In Zarasai, the 2026 minimum wage of EUR 1,153 is about 70% of the local average, so a large share of regional workers likely earn at or near the minimum.
- Using different years, Vilnius County's GDP per capita (EUR 41,500 in 2024) is roughly twice CWL's (EUR 21,273 in 2023). Lithuania is effectively a two-speed economy: Vilnius has converged above the EU average, while the rest of the country lags well behind.
- Unmet needs: remote and hybrid work hubs and jobs in shrinking regional towns; transport links for commuting to regional centres; regional childcare and elder care (where outward migration of working-age people leaves older people isolated); and housing incentives for regions. The young-families subsidy exists but is heavily oversubscribed (see section 3).

- Current (2025–2026) regional unemployment and job-vacancy data were not retrieved. The OECD figure is from 2017.
- Regional poverty rates for 2024–2025 were not retrieved.

## 3. Housing: prices, rents, social housing, Soviet-era block renovation, young buyers

### Takeaway
Vilnius apartments cost roughly EUR 2,950–3,600/m² in early 2026, against about EUR 2,650–2,750/m² in Kaunas and Klaipėda (aggregator estimates). An average earner can buy only about 60–64 m² in Vilnius, against about 89–91 m² in Kaunas and Klaipėda. Social housing is about 0.8% of the stock, with around 10,000 households waiting, often for more than 5 years in big cities. The state young-family mortgage subsidy pot (EUR 5.6m in 2026) ran out within minutes. Renovation of Soviet-era blocks is far behind target (about 15% of multi-apartment buildings done), which leaves many households facing heating bills more than 2.5 times those of efficient buildings.

### Inferences
- At EUR 2,950–3,600/m², a 50 m² Vilnius flat costs about EUR 147,500–180,000. That is roughly 8–10 years of the average net wage (EUR 1,514/month, or about EUR 18,200/year; see section 5) for a single earner. Young single earners and single-income households in Vilnius are effectively priced out without family help.
- The young-family subsidy (EUR 5.6m, about 330 families supported, against about 1,000 applications in minutes) is symbolic relative to demand. This is a clear gap where a larger or differently designed instrument could help, such as shared-equity schemes, regional rental housing, or cooperative and limited-profit rental housing. Housing Europe profiles a co-op housing sector in Lithuania: [housingcoop.eu, Cooperative housing in Lithuania](https://housingcoop.eu/resources/countries-in-europe/lithuania).
- Social housing at 0.8% of the stock is among the smallest shares in the EU. With waits of more than 5 years, the gap is structural: Lithuania lacks an affordable rental sector between social housing and the private market.
- Renovation has two bottlenecks: owner-consent and decision-making rules in multi-apartment buildings, and slow pace (about 333 buildings a year in 2024, against a target near 500 a year). Possible solutions include one-stop-shop renovation facilitators and building administrators, and help for owners' associations to reach consensus. At EUR 160 against EUR 62 a month for the same 50 m², the savings from renovation are large enough to make the case to owners.

### Gaps

- No official 2025–2026 rent statistics were found (Statistics Lithuania or Registrų centras).
- No authoritative end-2025 national renovation count. The APVA portal figure is undated, and the "5,000 by 2030" target mentioned in some results could not be pinned to a source.
- Waiting-list data by city (Vilnius, Kaunas, Klaipėda) for 2025 were not retrieved.
- Details of the new 2026 immovable property tax changes for homeowners were not retrieved.

## 4. Cost of living: inflation, food prices, heating, electricity and energy poverty

### Takeaway
Inflation re-accelerated through 2026: 3.1% in January, 4.8% in March and about 6.1% CPI (5.6% HICP) in August, driven by energy, housing and transport. The European Commission links this partly to Middle East-related energy price rises. Food prices are up about 50% since 2020 (Bank of Lithuania). Food inflation in 2025 ran at about 4–7%, among the higher rates in the EU, though Romania led. The biggest household shock was heating: the 9% reduced VAT on district heating was abolished from January 2026, and Vilnius heating bills in January 2026 were about 60% higher year on year. About 20% of Lithuanians cannot keep their homes adequately warm (2023), and 31.6% of the poor (2025).

### Cited Findings
**Inflation:**
- Annual CPI inflation was 3.1% in Jan 2026 and 3.6% in Feb, then 4.8% in Mar 2026, the highest since Aug 2023. ### Inferences
- The January 2026 heating bill for a 50 m² flat in an unrenovated block (EUR 160) equals about 19% of a net minimum wage (about EUR 847) and about 21% of an average old-age pension (about EUR 750). Older people living alone in unrenovated Soviet-era blocks are the core energy-poverty group. The VAT change was a fiscal measure (see section 5) that fell regressively on them. Means-tested compensation catches some of them, but uptake and the threshold design were not evaluated in any source found.
- With inflation back near 6% in late 2026 and energy-led, real gains from the 2026 minimum-wage and pension increases are being eroded. Inflation fell in early 2026 because food inflation eased, but energy, housing and transport then took over as drivers.
- Food: the problem is the price level (about +50% since 2020) and a concentrated five-chain market, not just the current rate. Price-comparison tools exist (ZUM monitoring, Pricer, Raskakcija). Possible gaps: the unexplored potential of short supply chains, farmers' cooperatives and social supermarkets; and stronger competition enforcement.

## 5. Taxes, wages, pensions, the second pillar and the shadow economy

### Takeaway
The 2026 tax reform (adopted 26 June 2025, effective 1 Jan 2026) introduced a three-bracket income tax (20/25/32%), raised corporate tax, and ended the reduced VAT on heating, largely to fund defence. The minimum wage is EUR 1,153 gross (about EUR 847 net) in 2026, against an average of about EUR 2,550 gross (about EUR 1,500–1,600 net). Pensions were indexed about 9.9% in 2026 to an average of about EUR 750, but replacement rates remain among the lowest in the OECD. The 2nd-pillar reform let more than 500,000 people withdraw over EUR 3bn (about 3% of GDP), and pension-fund assets fell by more than a third. The shadow economy is still about 20–25% of GDP.

### Inferences
- The minimum wage is about 45% of the average gross wage (1,153 / 2,554). An average pension of about EUR 750 is about 50% of the average net wage and only modestly above the 2024 single-person poverty line (EUR 616). Pensioners below the average, especially those with short or partly informal work records, fall below the poverty line. That matches the 37–40% AROP among older people and retirees.
- Higher-income earners mostly carry the burden of the new income tax brackets, but the end of the heating VAT relief is a broad consumption tax that falls hardest on poor and older households in old buildings. On net, the 2026 package probably did little to make the tax-benefit system more redistributive at the bottom. This is an inference; no distributional analysis was retrieved.
- Second-pillar withdrawals: young people withdrew most, and 72% of the money sits in bank accounts while inflation is about 5–6%. Two effects follow: (a) future retirement-income adequacy is reduced for a generation that already faces low state-pension replacement rates; (b) about EUR 2bn of idle savings is losing real value. There is a clear gap for independent financial guidance and low-cost voluntary saving products (3rd pillar).
- Envelope wages (18% of wages hidden) directly lower workers' future Sodra pensions and benefit entitlements, which ties informality to future old-age poverty.

## 6. Household debt, consumer credit and financial literacy

### Takeaway
Household debt is growing fast: Bank of Lithuania data show about EUR 17.8bn in Q1 2025 and about EUR 20.9bn in Q1 2026, roughly +18% y/y, driven mainly by mortgages. Consumer loans are expensive (8.6% average rate on new consumption loans in August 2025). Recent data on non-bank, payday and peer-to-peer lending and on financial literacy were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Bank of Lithuania household debt series (including non-profits serving households), EUR m: 15,994 → 16,359 → 16,829 → 17,268 → 17,768 → 18,473 → 19,370 → 20,073 → 20,892. The search summary maps this to Q1 2024 through Q1 2026, so about EUR 17.8bn in Q1 2025 and EUR 20.9bn in Q1 2026. — [Bank of Lithuania, Households debt](https://www.lb.lt/en/households-debt)
- In August 2025 the average rate on new euro loans to households was 5.30% overall: 8.58% for consumption loans and 3.68% for house purchase. — [Bank of Lithuania, lending rates](https://www.lb.lt/en/lending-rates-1)
- Consumption loans from MFIs were EUR 1,698.1m in Aug 2025 and EUR 1,799.7m in Dec 2025 (series definition per Bank of Lithuania). — [Bank of Lithuania, loans to households by purpose](https://www.lb.lt/en/loans-to-households-by-purpose-and-original-maturity). Bank consumer credit was EUR 3.23bn in Dec 2025, slightly down on November (different series). — [TheGlobalEconomy](https://www.theglobaleconomy.com/Lithuania/consumer_credit/)
- Consumer credit can be granted by banks, credit unions, peer-to-peer platforms and listed non-bank lenders. The latest non-bank portfolio figure found is from **2019**: EUR 715m and 421,000 customers (old). — [Bank of Lithuania, consumer credit providers](https://www.lb.lt/en/consumer-credit-providers-1)
- The responsible-lending change (first-time buyer down payment from 15% to 10%, Aug 2025) loosens mortgage access. See section 3.

### Inferences
- Debt growth accelerated from about +11% (Q1 2024 to Q1 2025) to about +18% (Q1 2025 to Q1 2026). That is consistent with falling mortgage rates, the looser down-payment rule and rising house prices. The household-level risk is concentrated among young mortgage borrowers in Vilnius, who also withdrew 2nd-pillar savings at the highest rates.
- Consumer credit at about 8.6% with inflation near 6% is not extreme at bank level. The likely hardship lies in non-bank and short-term lending, where no recent data were found. Debt advice and counselling services (free, independent, possibly municipal or NGO-run) look like a candidate gap, but this session found no evidence on how much of such provision exists.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 data on **payday or non-bank lending** (volumes, rates, arrears), on over-indebtedness or arrears rates, or on household debt-to-income were found.
- No **financial literacy** survey results (for example Bank of Lithuania or OECD/INFE) were found.
- No data on Bank of Lithuania consumer-protection enforcement or on the number of insolvencies or personal bankruptcies.

## 7. What Lithuanians say are the biggest problems (polls 2025–2026) and the political backdrop

### Takeaway
Every available poll puts **rising prices and inflation** first among Lithuanians' concerns. One Eurobarometer wave found inflation the top national problem at 39%, ahead of defence and security (26%) and Russia's war on Ukraine (20%). In 2023 the figure was 52%. Low wages, emigration, energy costs and social protection and health also feature. No Vilmorus or Spinter "most important problems" poll for 2025–2026 was found. ## 8. Government programmes, municipal initiatives and social enterprises: what works, what fails, where new solutions are needed

### Takeaway
Lithuania relies on a set of means-tested cash supports: heating compensation, rent compensation, social assistance and a small social-housing stock. It also uses large across-the-board increases (minimum wage +11.1% and pension indexation +9.87% in 2026) and capital programmes such as block renovation and the young-family mortgage subsidy. EU and OECD assessments say these transfers reduce poverty less than in most EU countries. Several programmes are badly under-scaled relative to demand: the young-family subsidy ran out in minutes, social housing waits run over 5 years, and renovation is about 15% complete. Little independent evidence was found on social enterprises or NGO initiatives.

### Inferences: concrete gaps where new solutions could help
1. **Energy poverty in unrenovated blocks:** heating costs EUR 160/month for 50 m² in unrenovated blocks against EUR 62 in efficient ones. The renovation pace (about 333 buildings in 2024) and the owner-consent rules are bottlenecks. Opportunities: renovation facilitation services for owners' associations, low-income bridging finance, and simple consumption feedback and metering-advice tools for older residents.
2. **Older people living alone:** AROP is about 37–40%, average pensions are about EUR 750, and the replacement rate is among the lowest in the OECD. Opportunities: targeted pension top-ups (a policy choice), outreach to raise take-up of compensations (the take-up gap is unmeasured), and services that cut costs such as shared meals, transport and home-care cooperatives.
3. **Affordable rental housing:** social housing is 0.8% of the stock and waits exceed 5 years. Opportunities: limited-profit or cooperative rental models (Housing Europe co-op framework), municipal leasing of private flats for rent-to-tenant schemes, and regional rental housing for workers.
4. **Young households' housing:** the subsidy is tiny relative to demand and Vilnius affordability is about 60–64 m². Opportunities: shared-equity or rent-to-own products, and scaling or redesigning the subsidy.
5. **Food costs:** prices are about +50% since 2020 in a concentrated market. Opportunities: transparent price-comparison tools (some exist), social supermarkets and food banks (effectiveness data not found), and farmer-to-consumer short supply chains.
6. **Pension savings after the 2nd-pillar exit:** about EUR 2bn of withdrawn savings sits idle in bank accounts, losing real value at about 5–6% inflation. Opportunities: independent, non-commercial financial guidance and low-fee voluntary savings defaults.
7. **Regional opportunity gap:** Zarasai earnings are about 62% of Vilnius's. Opportunities: remote-work hubs and regional employer incentives. A plausible further gap is regional childcare and elder care, but this session found no data to assess it.
8. **Informality:** 18% of wages are paid in envelopes. This lowers workers' future pensions, and awareness tools that show workers what envelope wages cost their pensions could help.
